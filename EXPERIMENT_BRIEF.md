# Experiment Brief: Show vs. Tell in Prose

## Source
art_01M6Q8W5S3ASWTWKS200QHGFVD, revision 1 — Parr's advice:
"characters should reveal their traits by their actions and talk;
young authors often write dialogue that is very good, fresh and
crisp... rarely when they turn to digressing, explaining and
analysing motives."

## What is held constant
- Same two characters, same story event (helping with a stop)
- Same length (approx. 60–65 words each)
- Same narrative tense and viewpoint (close third)
- No genre signals, no framing or title shown to readers

## What is changed
- Version A: traits stated by narrator (expository)
- Version B: traits shown through observed action and two-line dialogue

## Discriminating test
Show each version to separate readers (3–5 per version).
After reading, ask — without prompting trait words:
  1. "What can you tell me about Mira?"
  2. "What did she do?"
  3. "How confident are you in your description?"

Record exact words used. Count whether readers volunteer
an evaluative label (kind, nosy, helpful) or a behavioural
description (she watched, she said she'd tell him).

## Competing explanations to watch for
- A produces faster agreement on traits (clarity) but less
  reader investment (they were told, not shown).
- B produces richer or more varied descriptions (engagement)
  but possibly lower confidence or more divergent readings.
- Both versions may produce the same labels — if so, the
  mode may matter less than Parr implies for this event type.

## Information-gain criterion
The test is informative if A and B produce measurably
different patterns in question 1 responses. If they do not,
that is the more interesting result: it would narrow where
the technique matters, not confirm that it always does.
